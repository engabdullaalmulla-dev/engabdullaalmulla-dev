# The player this is aimed at

Source: r/CozyGamers, "Mobile game with no ads/no time requirement?", u/Francl27, ~2 years
old, archived. 16 upvotes, 26 comments. One person describing what they want, with a
couple of dozen people agreeing or suggesting. That is a hypothesis generator, not
market validation — but it is a very specific hypothesis, and it is worth reading
literally rather than through the word "cozy".

## Line by line

| What they said | What it constrains |
|---|---|
| "I like story/cooking/farming/building/tycoon games" | Genre confirmed. Not puzzle. |
| "don't want… wait 4 hours for something to build" | No energy timers, no build queues |
| "or… use special currency or bonuses to actually be able to beat a level" | Difficulty may never be paywalled |
| "none of those 'need friends to advance' game either" | No social gating, no invites |

| **"I liked the dash series but they were finished pretty fast"** | **The reference title is Diner Dash, and the complaint is length** |
| "something I can play a few hours a week without feeling that I have to play" | No daily streaks, no login rewards, no FOMO |
| "No forced ads please." | No ads, interstitial or rewarded-as-mandatory |

## The line that changes the design

> "would like something that lasts a while (like I liked the dash series but they were
> finished pretty fast)"

The Dash series *is* the time-pressure genre. Diner Dash is the game where customers sit
down, get impatient, and you run food to them against the clock — the direct ancestor of
Cooking Fever and every frenzy clone since.

So this person is not asking for a calm game. They liked the fast one. What they rejected
was never the *pace*; it was the **free-to-play scaffolding** (waits, premium currency,
friend-gating, ads) and the **length**.

That matters twice over:

1. **The rush prototype is closer to this brief than the calm one.** I said last time that
   pivoting to a frenzy loop created tension with this request. Re-reading the actual
   post, that was wrong. The tension is with the frenzy genre's *monetisation*, not with
   its pacing. This person would like a Dash game. They would like it to be longer and
   not to be nickel-and-dimed.
2. **"Lasts a while" is the headline feature, and neither prototype has it.** The single
   most-stated want in the post is longevity, and it is named as the specific failure of
   the closest existing product. A seven-day campaign is exactly the thing they already
   complained about finishing too fast.

## What the top comment adds

The most-upvoted reply recommends Kairosoft — premium-priced pixel-art management sims —
and the commenter notes they own about five of them. Different person from the poster, so
weight it accordingly, but it points at a real pattern: this audience does buy paid games
outright, and buys them repeatedly from a developer they trust.

Kairosoft's longevity does not come from content volume. It comes from deep interlocking
systems that reward optimisation, so the game outlives its own content. That is the
cheaper route to "lasts a while" for a small team than simply authoring more levels.

## Where the current plan diverges from the post

**Price.** The post says "don't mind paying a few bucks to avoid ads" — call it USD 3–7,
roughly AED 11–26 — and frames it as *removing ads from a free game*, not as buying a
premium title. The plan's AED 29.99–39.99 is USD 8–11, above what this specific reference
player signalled, and against a different mental model. That is not fatal: the Kairosoft
comment shows the audience pays more than "a few bucks" when the value is legible. But
the price should be tested as its own question rather than assumed from the post.

**Length.** A first version of one café, eight recipes and a dozen customers is the right
*build* scope. It is not a sufficient *value* proposition for the one thing this player
asked for most. Those are different problems and need separate answers — see below.

## How to last a while without FOMO mechanics

Longevity normally comes from retention mechanics this player has explicitly rejected. The
routes that remain:

1. **Systems that outlive their content.** The Kairosoft answer. Upgrades that interact,
   menu combinations with emergent trade-offs, layouts that change how a shift plays.
   Replay value from optimisation rather than from new assets.
2. **An endless mode.** The cheapest possible test of whether the loop has legs past the
   scripted campaign, and the standard answer in this genre. Now in the rush prototype:
   unlocked after day three, one continuous shift that ramps every forty seconds until
   three customers walk out, with a personal best to beat.
3. **Many short, differently-constrained levels.** Cook, Serve, Delicious! ships hundreds.
   Expensive in authoring, cheap in tech.
4. **New verbs, not bigger numbers.** An order needing two machines, a customer who
   changes their mind, a rush hour where the menu is forced. This is what keeps day
   twenty from being day four with a faster clock.

Routes 1 and 2 are where a solo developer gets the most length per unit of work. Route 3
is what your expansion packs should be.

## What to actually test

The post gives a sharp falsification target, so use it:

- Hand someone the rush build. After they stop, ask what would make them open it again
  next week. If the honest answer is "nothing", no amount of content fixes it.
- Then ask what they would pay for it. Do not lead with a number.

Those two answers settle the length question and the price question, which are the two
places the current plan is furthest from the person it is aimed at.
